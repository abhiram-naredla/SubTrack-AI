For SubTrack AI, the most important metric isn’t just traffic — it’s whether companies actually discover meaningful savings opportunities. The main thing we’d track is how many completed audits identify at least $300/month in potential savings. That tells us people are not only using the product, but also finding real financial value in it.

We’d also pay close attention to how many visitors actually complete the audit flow. If users drop off early, it usually means the onboarding is confusing, too long, or not convincing enough. Another important signal is how many people choose to submit their email after seeing the results. That helps measure whether the audit feels useful and trustworthy enough for users to want future reports or optimization alerts.

The average savings identified per audit also matters a lot. Higher savings estimates naturally make the product more shareable and increase the chances that teams will take action or book a consultation. Speaking of sharing, tracking how often audit links get revisited or shared with teammates can show whether the reports are genuinely useful inside companies rather than just a one-time curiosity.

Eventually, the strongest business metric would probably be how many users convert into optimization calls or consulting conversations, since that shows real monetization potential beyond just free audits.

There are also a few metrics we intentionally wouldn’t focus on too much. Raw pageviews don’t mean much if people never finish the audit. Time spent on the website can actually be a bad sign if users are confused. Even total registered users isn’t that important early on because the goal is to attract qualified startups with real AI spend problems, not inflate signup numbers.

Long term, the product could evolve into something more continuous — tracking AI spending over time, helping teams reduce subscription waste, monitoring infrastructure savings, and improving retention as companies scale their AI stack.
